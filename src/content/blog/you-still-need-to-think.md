Last month, one of my students told me she had "hired a website" three times before joining my class. She'd paid a freelancer who disappeared, paid another who delivered something she couldn't edit, and then tried a drag-and-drop builder and gave up in the templates tab.

Then, in week three of *Build Your First Website with AI*, she shipped a booking site for her salon. Her clients use it today.

Nothing about that story is magic, and it's not really a story about AI either. It's a story about **thinking**.

## The prompt is not the product

When people hear "build a website with AI," they imagine typing one sentence and receiving a finished product. And honestly? You can get *something* that way — a generic page that looks like everyone else's generic page.

The gap between "AI made me a website" and "I made a website with AI" is exactly one thing: **decisions before prompts**.

Before any of my students open an AI tool, I make them answer five questions in plain language:

1. Who is this website for?
2. What is the *one action* you want a visitor to take?
3. What are the three things they need to see before they'll take it?
4. What do you want people to feel in the first five seconds?
5. What will you say when someone asks "how much / how do I book / where are you"?

Notice that not one of those questions is technical. That's the point. AI has absorbed the *technical* layer of web development — syntax, boilerplate, layout mechanics. It has not absorbed *your judgment*. It never will, because your judgment is the one thing that isn't in the training data.

## What the machine is actually good at

I've been a professional engineer for ten years. I've built booking engines, government identity systems, USSD platforms for farmers. And I'll be honest about what AI has changed for building websites:

- **Scaffolding is solved.** A sensible page structure, clean navigation, a responsive layout — that used to be days of work. It's now a good prompt away.
- **Iteration is cheap.** "Make the button green, move the booking form above the reviews, add a WhatsApp link" — each of those used to be a developer ticket. Now it's a sentence.
- **Deployment is almost fun.** Free hosting, one-click deploys, custom domains. The scary part of "putting it on the internet" is gone.

And what AI is *not* good at, at least not without you:

- Knowing what your customers actually care about
- Telling your story in a way that sounds like you and not like a brochure
- Deciding what to leave *out*
- Noticing that the whole thing is technically fine and strategically wrong

## The workflow I teach

So in my classes, the order of operations is deliberately boring:

1. **Plan on paper.** The five questions above. Ten minutes, no computer.
2. **Write the brief, not the prompt.** A short document describing sections, tone, and the one action. My students write briefs *before* they ever talk to an AI.
3. **Generate, then judge.** We generate, then we put on our "grumpy customer" hat and critique what came back. This judging step is the actual skill.
4. **Edit in small loops.** One change at a time, checking each on a real phone. Small loops keep you in control; giant "fix everything" prompts hand control back to the machine.
5. **Ship, then improve.** Publish before it's perfect. Websites are gardens, not statues.

That's it. No secrets. The people who struggle most in my classes are never the ones without "tech skills" — they're the ones who skip the planning step because generating feels like progress.

## The uncomfortable, liberating truth

If you run a business, the barrier to having a professional website is no longer money or code. It's twenty honest minutes with a notebook, the willingness to let an AI do the mechanical work, and the discipline to stay the one making the decisions.

The code was never the hard part anyway. It was always the thinking.

That's what I teach now — and honestly, watching a caterer out-ship a marketing agency with a brief and a phone is my favorite plot twist of this whole AI era.
